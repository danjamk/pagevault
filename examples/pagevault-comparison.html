Sharing a private HTML report is a solved problem. Choosing the right tool isn't.

A dozen tools turn an AI-made HTML file into a link only the right people can open. They are not interchangeable — each is optimizing for something different. This guide sorts them by what you actually care about, and states plainly where each one wins.

Where we fit

This guide was made by PageVault. Here's our hand, face up.

On the map above, PageVault leads two frames — the collection and artifact handling — and sits mid-pack or lower on every other one. We're not the most private (Stacktree is), the most accountable (Peony is), or the most collaborative (sharehtml is). That's fine. We're aiming at a different square.

Almost every other tool treats the link as the unit: publish a file, get a URL, done. PageVault treats the client as the unit — every artifact lands in one durable portal, gated to their email, that they bookmark once. No single capability below is ours alone anymore; data rooms have collections, five tools have an MCP server. What's rare is the shape of the whole thing:

What's genuinely rare here

  • The whole combination. Free, self-hosted on your own domain, renders the artifact and Markdown, keeps it as a per-client collection, open-source enough to read in a sitting. Any one piece you can find elsewhere; the set is the gap.
  • Agent read-back of the collection. An MCP server lets Claude search and read a client's whole corpus later — "what did we decide about the CDC migration in March?" The nearest rival, Peony, has a bidirectional MCP, but it's a $30+/mo data room, not a free deliverable portal.

When to skip PageVault

  • The artifact is confidential. Watermarks, NDA gates, named audit → Peony.
  • The host must never see plaintext → Stacktree's client-side encryption.
  • You want live feedback on the doc → jonesphillip/sharehtml.
  • You need a CRM, invoicing, e-sign → SuiteDash or Copilot.
  • You have under ~5 clients. A shared folder per client is genuinely faster until the artifacts pile up.
